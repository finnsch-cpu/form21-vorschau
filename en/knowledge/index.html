Artificial intelligence (AI)Draft

An umbrella term for systems that infer from their inputs how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions. Many of today’s AI systems learn from data rather than following hard-coded rules.

ChatbotDraft

A program you can have a conversation with in writing or by voice. Many current chatbots are based on large language models. Under the EU AI Act, people generally need to be able to tell that they are interacting with an AI system.

Context windowDraft

The amount of text a language model can take into account at once, measured in tokens. Anything that no longer fits, such as the start of a very long chat, can no longer be taken into account.

Generative AIDraft

AI that can generate content such as text, images or audio. For example, it can turn a few bullet points into a draft invitation.

HallucinationDraft

A false or invented detail in an AI output that can sound convincing. One example is a citation to a research paper that does not exist. Check important claims against the original source.

Language modelDraft

An AI model that learns patterns in language during training. Many language models generate text by predicting, step by step, which piece of text, called a token, could come next.

LLM (large language model)Draft

LLM stands for “large language model”. These models are trained on large amounts of data and can, for example, answer questions, summarise texts or write drafts.

Machine learningDraft

A branch of AI in which a system learns patterns from example data instead of being given every rule. One example is a spam filter that learns what spam looks like from many labelled emails.

Multimodal AIDraft

AI that can process or generate several kinds of content, such as text, images, audio or video. For example, it can read a photo of flipchart notes and turn it into a written summary.

PromptDraft

A question, instruction or task you give an AI system. For example: “Summarise these notes in three bullet points for my team.”

TokenDraft

A piece of text that a language model processes. Depending on the model, a token can be a whole word, part of a word or a punctuation mark. A long word can be split into several tokens.

AI Literacy & the EU AI Act

AI literacyDraft

The knowledge and skills to make informed use of AI and assess its opportunities and risks. This includes checking AI outputs before using them in your work. Article 4 of the EU AI Act addresses AI literacy.

EU AI ActDraft

The EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, sets rules for AI in the European Union. Obligations depend in part on how the system is used, the risk involved and whether an organisation provides or uses it.

Original text (eur-lex.europa.eu) (external website)

High-risk AI systemDraft

An AI system that the EU AI Act classifies as particularly risky. This includes certain uses in education and employment, such as assessing learning outcomes or selecting job applicants. Such systems carry obligations including risk management, documentation and human oversight.

Human oversightDraft

People monitor how an AI system is used and can intervene. In practice, someone might check, revise or discard an AI-generated draft before sending a reply to a customer.

Prohibited AI practicesDraft

AI applications that Article 5 of the EU AI Act bans because their risk is considered unacceptable. They include manipulative techniques, social scoring and emotion recognition in the workplace and in education, except for medical or safety reasons.

Providers and deployersDraft

Two roles in the EU AI Act. Providers develop an AI system, or have one developed, and place it on the market under their own name. Deployers use an AI system under their own authority, for example a company that gives its team an AI assistant. Each role comes with different obligations.

Risk-based approachDraft

The core principle of the EU AI Act: the higher the risk an AI use poses to health, safety and fundamental rights, the stricter the rules. Some practices are banned, high-risk systems carry extensive obligations, and others mainly face transparency obligations or no specific requirements.

Transparency obligationsDraft

Requirements in the EU AI Act that help people recognise AI. Anyone talking to a chatbot should generally be aware of it. AI-generated content should be marked in a machine-readable way, and deepfakes must be disclosed.

BiasDraft

A systematic distortion in AI outputs. It often stems from one-sided training data or assumptions made during development. Bias can put certain groups at a disadvantage, for example when a system rates applications from some people lower than others.

DeepfakeDraft

Images, video or audio generated or altered with AI that convincingly imitate real people, places or events. Under the EU AI Act, it must be disclosed that such content has been artificially generated or manipulated.

Environmental footprint of AIDraft

The environmental impact of AI across its life cycle, such as the electricity and water data centres use for training and for every request, and the manufacture of hardware. Using AI deliberately can help keep this footprint down.

Responsible AIDraft

An approach to developing and using AI that takes fairness, transparency, data protection, safety and sustainability into account. In organisations this can mean agreeing clear rules for using AI and checking outputs critically.

Training dataDraft

Examples an AI model learns patterns from during training, such as texts or images. The examples used in training influence what the model does well and where it is more likely to make mistakes.

Prompt Engineering & AI Agents

Power users who want to use AI not just as a writing aid but for efficient workflows.

View module
AI agentDraft

An AI system that works towards a goal in several steps and chooses what to do next based on interim results. It can use tools, such as search, to find sources and put together an overview.

AI assistantDraft

An AI chat you set up for a recurring task, with your own instructions and, if needed, your own documents. Examples include custom GPTs, Claude Projects and Gemini Gems.

No-codeDraft

Tools that let you build applications or workflows without programming skills, for example by connecting building blocks with your mouse. They also make it possible to add AI steps to everyday work.

Prompt engineeringDraft

Deliberately writing, testing and refining prompts so that an AI system reliably produces useful results. This can include a clear role, context, examples and a desired output format.

System promptDraft

Standing instructions that apply to every request in a chat or AI assistant, for example about tone, task or limits. For example: “Always answer in plain language and name your sources.”

Workflow automationDraft

A chain of work steps that runs automatically when a trigger occurs. For example: a new enquiry arrives through a form, an AI step summarises it and the team gets a message. Tools for this include n8n.

General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)Draft

The EU regulation that protects personal data. It also applies when you enter such data into AI tools, such as names or email addresses from customer enquiries. So agree in advance which data may go into which tool.

Personal dataDraft

Information about a person who can be identified directly or indirectly. Examples include a name or a personal email address in a customer enquiry.
